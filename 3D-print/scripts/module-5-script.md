## SEGMENT 1: SUPPORT REMOVAL & CLEANUP (~15 min)

### [INTRO]
In Segment 1, you'll learn professional techniques for removing every trace of 3D printing supports without damaging your frame's finished surfaces. We'll cover tool selection, safe removal methods for different support types, witness mark cleanup, and the critical inspection techniques that separate A-grade frames from rejects. By the end of this segment, you'll have a repeatable process that takes roughly 10 to 15 minutes per frame, and you'll know exactly what to look for under critical lighting to catch imperfections before they leave your workshop.

### [SCRIPT]

Let's talk about the reality of 3D printing: every single frame that comes off your build plate is going to have support material attached to it. A lot of people see those supports and think, "Well, maybe I just snap them off and call it done." That's actually the fastest way to ruin a product that you're about to sell to a customer.

I've been printing these frames for six years across six different brands, and I can tell you that support removal is where most people either become professionals or stay as hobbyists. The difference is patience, the right tools, and understanding physics.

Let's start with the tools, because this is non-negotiable. You need a set of flush cutters — I use the Hakko CHP-170, about eight dollars on Amazon. These aren't regular wire cutters; the blade is specifically ground to cut flat against a surface without leaving a huge stub. If you use regular side cutters or diagonal cutters, you're going to leave a visible nub that you'll have to sand forever. That's time, that's cost, and it's unnecessary. You also need a hobby knife, specifically an X-Acto #1 with fresh blades — dull blades slip and cause injury. Keep a needle-nose pliers for gripping support bases, and have your sandpaper set ready: 150-grit, 220-grit, and 400-grit.

Now, here's where most people make their first critical mistake. They try to rip supports off like they're opening a can of soda. That doesn't work because the layer bonding is strong. What you need to do is score the junction first. Take your hobby knife, hold it at about a 35-degree angle pointing away from the frame itself — this is important; you're always cutting away from the part. Make a shallow cut right at the base of the support. You're not gouging; you're just scoring the line where the support meets the frame. This breaks the bond slightly. Then you can snap it off cleanly.

Different support types require different approaches. Tree supports, which are the newer style, should peel off by hand if you score them properly. Normal line supports need your pliers. Grip the base of the support as close to the junction as possible, then twist — don't just pull. The twisting motion breaks the bond. Grid supports under the frame are stubborn; those need flush cutters because you need to cut from multiple angles. Cut as close to the surface as your blade allows, then flip the frame and cut from the other side, and you'll minimize the nub left behind.

After removal, you're left with witness marks — those little rough spots where the support was attached. This is where 220-grit sandpaper on a flat sanding block comes in. I keep a dedicated flat block for my workshop; mine is a 3x6 inch piece of MDF. You put the frame on the table, place your sandpaper block flat against the witness mark, and use light, circular motions. The key word is light. If you press hard, you'll sand through the frame's outer wall. You're just smoothing the texture. Typically, this takes 30 seconds per witness mark.

Now, here's something that separates professionals from everyone else: elephant's foot correction. If your first layer squished, you might have a slightly domed or rough bottom. Professional frames sit flat on a fridge, not tilted. Grab your 220-grit sandpaper, place it flat on your table, and sand the bottom of your frame in a figure-eight motion. You're checking if the frame sits flush; if it rocks, keep sanding. This takes maybe 20 seconds and it's invisible to the customer, but it's the difference between a stable product and one that tilts on their fridge.

Finally, inspection under critical lighting. I use a 5000K LED panel — cool white, bright. Hold the frame at eye level, slowly rotate it 360 degrees, and look for any remaining bumps, scratches, or imperfections. This is your last catch point before painting. If you miss something here, it'll be visible in the painted product, and the customer will see it.

## SEGMENT 2: SANDING TECHNIQUES (~20 min)

### [INTRO]
Segment 2 is all about advanced sanding strategies that take your frame from functional to premium. You'll learn the grit progression system, directional sanding rules that prevent cross-grain scratches, specialized techniques for magnet pockets and photo cavities, and the specific areas where sanding actually hurts your product. We'll also cover wet sanding with PLA for an ultra-smooth finish, dust safety, and realistic time budgets for different frame styles so you know exactly how long each frame should take.

### [SCRIPT]

Here's something I learned the hard way: not all parts of a frame should be sanded equally. A lot of beginners sand everything aggressively, which eats away at fine details and takes forever. What we're doing here is selective, strategic sanding.

First, let's talk about when you actually need to sand. If your nozzle temperature was dialed in correctly and your layer height was 0.20 millimeters or less, the layer lines might be invisible. If you can't see them at arm's length — that's about 60 centimeters away — you don't need to sand that surface. You do need to sand if the brim edges are rough, if there are visible layer lines, or if the magnet pocket is slightly too tight for your magnet. Don't sand for the sake of sanding. Time is money.

The grit progression is your framework. Think of it like this: each grit has a job. 150-grit is aggressive; it removes bumps and visibly shapes the surface. You only use this on parts that clearly need it — rough brim edges, major print imperfections. 220-grit is your workhorse; this smooths general surfaces and is what you'll use 80 percent of the time. 400-grit is for polishing visible faces under studio light; it's the final touch before painting.

Now, here's a rule that I can't overemphasize: always sand in the direction of the layer lines, never perpendicular. Layer lines run horizontally on most frame designs. If you sand perpendicular to them — cross-grain — the sandpaper teeth catch the edge of each layer and create scratches that look worse than the original layer lines. If you sand parallel, you're smoothing each layer line gently. It's the difference between sanding oak with the grain versus against the grain.

Wet sanding is a game-changer for 400-grit work. You're working with PLA, which can generate a lot of fine dust and the sandpaper clogs easily. Fill a small bowl with water, dip your 400-grit sandpaper in it every 10 seconds, and sand the visible faces. The water prevents clogging, the friction is smoother, and you get a finish that's noticeably silkier. This takes maybe an extra 30 seconds per frame, but the result is genuinely different.

Here's a critical mistake I see constantly: people sand inside the photo cavity. Don't do this. The photo print — whether it's Instax, Polaroid, or standard 4x6 — needs to glide into that cavity smoothly. If you create a textured surface inside, the photo will catch and the customer won't be able to insert or remove it easily. The inside of the cavity must stay smooth. The only exception is if the cavity is slightly too tight and you need to enlarge it by 0.1 millimeter; in that case, wrap 220-grit around a 5-millimeter dowel, insert it into the cavity, and rotate gently. You're enlarging, not texturing.

Same rule applies to registration pin surfaces. These are the small alignment posts that help the back plate snap on correctly. If you sand those, they wear unevenly and the back plate won't snap properly. Leave them as-printed.

Let me give you a realistic time budget. An Instax simple frame — just a flat back and a flat front — takes about 2 to 3 minutes of sanding if you need any at all. A Retro TV frame with curved edges and accent details takes 5 to 8 minutes because you have more surface area and more detail to preserve. A Collage frame with multiple photo cavities and complex geometry takes 8 to 12 minutes. If you're spending 30 minutes sanding a single frame, you're either over-sanding or you printed badly. One or the other needs to change.

Dust safety is important. PLA dust is biologically inert, but breathing it all day gets annoying and irritates your throat. If you're doing a sanding session longer than 30 minutes, put on an N95 mask and work in a well-ventilated space. I have a small exhaust fan in my workshop that pulls air away from my face when I'm sanding. It's not complicated; it's just necessary.

Before you move to painting, do a before-and-after demo on one frame. Look at an unsanded section under bright studio light, then look at a sanded section. The difference is dramatic, and that teaches you whether you've sanded enough or if you're overdoing it.

## SEGMENT 3: PAINT & COLOR FINISHING (~25 min)

### [INTRO]
Segment 3 shows you how to turn a grey 3D-printed frame into a polished, professional product that commands premium prices. You'll learn spray paint setup, primer and top coat selection, the sweeping spray motion that prevents drips, color psychology that sells frames, specialized techniques like multi-color frames and color-change-at-layer printing, clear coating for durability, and how to price different finishes. By the end, you'll understand why a matte black finish is your best seller and how to execute two-tone frames that sell at 2x the standard price.

### [SCRIPT]

Let's be direct: paint is where you transform a plastic object into a product people want to buy. A raw 3D-printed frame looks like a toy. A painted frame looks like something worth $18 to $25 on an Shopify shelf.

Setup is everything. I've been through a lot of spray paint failures, and they were all due to lazy booth setup. You don't need a professional booth; you need a cheap cardboard spray booth, a lazy susan turntable, and a flat surface away from wind. Your total investment is about $15: a folding card table ($12 at Costco), a cardboard box spray booth ($3 on Amazon), and a plastic lazy susan ($3). That's it.

The booth is simple: you cut two opposite sides out of a cardboard box, leaving the top and three sides intact. This traps overspray and keeps your garage clean. The lazy susan goes inside; you place your frame on the lazy susan. This allows you to rotate the frame while spraying without touching it, which prevents fingerprints. Before you spray anything, lay down newspaper under the booth and put on a respirator. I'm not kidding about this. Spray paint fumes are no joke; a half-face respirator with organic vapor cartridges is $25 and non-negotiable.

Now, primer. Always primer. Spray paint doesn't bond well directly to PLA; primer drastically improves adhesion. I use Rust-Oleum 2X Primer in either grey or white, about $5 a can at Home Depot. Two light coats. Light is the critical word. If you spray a heavy coat, it drips. Two light coats look perfect, dry faster, and don't drip. Between coats, wait exactly 10 minutes. Don't spray too soon; the first coat won't be dry. Don't wait hours; it doesn't matter.

Here's the spray motion that separates professionals from everyone else: hold the can 25 to 30 centimeters away from the frame — that's about 10 to 12 inches. Spray in smooth, parallel, sweeping motions. You're moving the can back and forth at a consistent distance; you're not pointing and holding the button down at one spot. Beginners always spray at a single spot, which causes drips and puddles. You're painting as if you're mowing a lawn: parallel passes, slight overlap, consistent motion. If you feel like you're not getting enough paint on, you're doing it right. Two light coats are always better than one heavy coat.

Color selection is business strategy. Matte black is THE number one seller. Why? It hides layer lines, hides printing imperfections, hides dust and fingerprints, and looks premium. I sell probably 60 percent matte black frames. Use Rust-Oleum Matte Black 2X. Metallics are the premium upsell: gold, rose gold, chrome silver. These are $3 more per can than standard colors, so they cost you maybe $0.50 more per frame in paint, but you charge $5 to $10 extra for metallics. Montana Gold spray paint has the best color range if you want custom shades. Krylon ColorMaxx is also solid and dries faster.

Here's where it gets interesting: multi-color frames. You print one frame, mask sections with painter's tape, spray one color, let it dry, remove the tape, mask different sections, spray a second color. You end up with a two-tone frame. These sell for double. A matte black frame with white accents, or white with rose-gold metallic, or navy with gold trim — these are premium products. Once you show a two-color frame on your Shopify listing, customers ask for them constantly.

If you want a color change at a specific layer height, you use Cura's "Pause at Height" plugin. In Cura, you set a pause at exact Z-height — for instance, at 20 millimeters up the frame. The printer pauses mid-print, you swap filament to a different color, and hit resume. The frame prints the rest in the new color. When it comes off the printer, you have a single-print two-color frame. This is advanced, but it's worth learning.

Clear coat is the final protective layer. Rust-Oleum 2X Clear in either matte or gloss — your choice based on the look you want. One thin coat, not two. Clear coat adds protection from fridge rubbing and UV exposure, which extends shelf life if a customer takes a photo and the frame sits on a sunny shelf. Drying is critical: minimum 2 hours between any coats, and 24 hours full cure before you insert magnets or assemble the frame. If you try to assemble too early, paint can stick to itself and peel.

Your station setup takes 15 minutes to assemble and costs maybe $15 in materials. Once it's set up, a frame goes from raw 3D print to fully painted and cleared in about 45 minutes (including drying time in between coats). That's scalable production.

## SEGMENT 4: MAGNET INSTALLATION PRO TIPS (~20 min)

### [INTRO]
Segment 4 is the most technically rigorous segment in the module because magnet polarity is unforgiving. You'll learn magnet grading (why N52 is non-negotiable), sizing standards for different frame types, polarity verification with a compass, the critical orientation standard that allows customers to stack frames without repelling, adhesive selection and application technique, flush mounting for professional appearance, and how to fix polarity mistakes if they happen. This segment separates frames that work reliably from frames that customers return because they won't stick to the fridge.

### [SCRIPT]

This is the segment where I'm going to be adamant about one thing: magnet polarity. I've sold magnets attached to frames made by other vendors, and I can tell you that about one in twenty frames has reversed polarity. The customer gets home, tries to stick it on their fridge, and nothing happens. Or worse, it repels. They open a support ticket. They're frustrated. They leave a bad review. All of this is preventable with 30 seconds of verification per frame.

Let's start with magnet selection. You have three main grades: N35, N42, and N52. These are neodymium magnets; the number represents strength. For our application — fridges — you use N52. Full stop. N35 is budget-grade and it's weak; a photo frame plus magnetized fridge might hold for a while, but a customer bumps it and it falls. N52 is the strongest commercially available neodymium. The cost difference is maybe $0.02 per magnet in bulk. There's no reason to cheap out.

Standard sizes are 6-millimeter diameter by 2-millimeter thick for Instax and Polaroid frames — these are light, and two to four magnets hold them easily. For heavier collage frames, I use 10-millimeter diameter by 2-millimeter thick. The larger size holds more weight. When you order magnets, order them in bulk: KJ Magnetics online for 100+ packs, or Amazon for smaller quantities. At 100-pack quantities, you're paying $0.08 to $0.12 per magnet. Over 500, it drops to $0.05. This is a line item that pays for itself.

Now, polarity. A magnet has a North pole and a South pole. The fridge is magnetic steel. When a magnet's North pole faces the fridge, it attracts. When it's reversed, it repels. The rule is this: all of your magnets, in all of your frames, must have the same pole facing outward — toward the fridge. Why? Because if a customer buys three frames from you and they want to stack them or overlap them on their fridge, they'll all attract each other. If one is reversed, it'll repel the others. That's a bad experience.

Here's how you verify polarity. Before inserting any magnet into a frame, take a compass. The compass needle points North. Hold your magnet next to the compass. The end of the magnet that attracts the needle is the North pole. Mark that end with a Sharpie dot. Do this for every magnet before you glue. I test on a reference magnet first — one that I know is correct — and then I use that reference magnet to verify the rest. Place your unmarked magnet near the reference. If North attracts North, they repel. If North attracts South, they attract. Mark accordingly.

The test against the fridge is simple: hold the magnet with the marked North pole facing the fridge. It should attract firmly and stick. Flip it 180 degrees so South faces the fridge. It should repel. Do this test. Actually put it on your fridge. If this test fails, your magnet is mislabeled or marked incorrectly. Fix it before you insert it into the frame.

Adhesive selection is critical. You need Loctite Super Glue Gel, not the liquid version. The gel stays in the magnet pocket; the liquid runs into the photo cavity and solidifies, which ruins the print slide area. A small bottle is about $4 and lasts through hundreds of magnets. Keep it fresh; once it's open, it starts to set. Buy new bottles frequently.

Application technique: take a toothpick, dab a dot of gel in the center of the magnet pocket — not around the edges, not a huge blob, just a small dot. Place the magnet with the correct pole facing outward. Use a 5-millimeter wooden dowel to press it in; don't use your fingers. Super glue bonds to skin instantly and you'll spend an ER visit explaining what happened. Press the dowel perpendicular to the magnet from the back, hold pressure for 30 seconds. The magnet sits flush, 0.1 to 0.2 millimeters below the frame surface. This is designed into the model; your pocket depth equals magnet thickness plus 0.1 millimeter.

How many magnets per frame? Instax and Polaroid simple frames: 2 magnets minimum. Medium frames like Retro TV or rectangular with slight weight: 4 magnets. Collage frames with multiple photos and more total weight: 6 magnets. Test the hold by placing the frame on a vertical fridge surface and applying gentle downward pressure. If it slides, add another magnet.

Curing time is 10 minutes for handling strength and 24 hours for full cure. Don't test on the fridge for at least 30 minutes. If you're in a hurry and you've glued incorrectly, you'd rather discover that now than have the magnet fall off when the customer picks up the frame.

If you absolutely reverse the polarity — glued it backwards and didn't notice until assembly — here's how you fix it. Take a soldering iron set to 250 degrees Celsius. Touch the tip to the PLA adjacent to the magnet pocket for 3 to 4 seconds. The PLA softens slightly. Use a dental pick to pry the magnet out gently. Clean the pocket, re-glue with correct polarity. Soldering iron damage is minimal if you don't linger; just soften, don't melt.

Sourcing at scale: KJ Magnetics is the best price for 100+ quantities. For 10 to 50 magnets, Amazon is fine. For 1 to 10, local hobby shops if you have one. Budget $0.08 to $0.12 per magnet in your cost calculations for all materials.

## SEGMENT 5: PHOTO INSERTION & FINAL ASSEMBLY (~15 min)

### [INTRO]
Segment 5 walks you through the final assembly steps that transform your completed frame into a finished product ready for shipment. You'll learn how to test photo fit, fix cavity sizing issues, handle different print types (Instax, Polaroid, standard 4x6), proper back plate assembly, and the sequence that prevents common mistakes. We'll also cover the fridge test that serves as your final quality gate before handing the product to customers.

### [SCRIPT]

Assembly is straightforward once you have all the pieces, but the sequence matters. I've made mistakes by rushing, and I've learned that doing things in the wrong order creates problems that are tedious to reverse.

Photo fit is your first test. Take the photo print — whether it's an Instax, Polaroid, or standard 4x6 — and slide it in from the top edge of the frame cavity. It should glide in smoothly. There's a sweet spot: not so tight that you struggle to insert it, and not so loose that it falls out when you flip the frame upside down. If it's too tight, the customer can't change photos. If it's too loose, photos fall inside the frame and you get a support ticket.

If the fit is too tight, here's the fix. Wrap 220-grit sandpaper around a flat popsicle stick. Insert it into the cavity along one wall. Sand gently, rotating the stick to sand all four walls. You're removing 0.1 millimeter at a time. Take it easy; aggressive sanding can create an undercut that weakens the cavity. Test after each light sanding. Usually, 20 to 30 seconds of gentle sanding per wall gets you to perfect.

If the fit is too loose, use thin foam adhesive tape. The kind used for weatherstripping is about 1 millimeter thick. Cut a strip and apply it to one interior wall of the cavity. This creates a gentle friction grip that holds the photo in place without being too tight. Test fit after applying; you might need to add it to a second wall.

Photo handling matters. For Instax prints, the glossy side faces outward; the white border is usually hidden by the cavity design, so the photo appears borderless in the finished frame. Standard 4x6 prints from CVS or Walgreens need to be trimmed. Use a paper cutter, not scissors. Scissors create wavy edges that look unprofessional. The cavity dimensions are usually exact, so trim the print to match.

Assembly sequencing: this is important. The wrong order creates a nightmare.

Step one: insert magnets into the back plate. The back plate is the part that snaps onto the main frame. Glue your magnets here first. Wait 30 minutes for the glue to cure completely.

Step two: once magnets are fully cured, insert the photo into the front frame cavity from the top edge.

Step three: align the back plate with the main frame. There are usually snap points or registration pins. Press down firmly; you'll hear or feel a click. The back should be secure.

At this point, your frame is complete: magnets installed, photo inserted, back plate snapped on.

The fridge test is your final quality gate. Place the assembled frame on a vertical fridge surface. The magnets should hold it firmly with minimal shift. Load the photo carefully; it should be visible, straight, and secure. Apply gentle downward pressure; the frame shouldn't tilt or slide. If something feels wrong, don't ship it. Disassemble, diagnose, and fix it.

Weight consideration: the total frame — plastic, photo, magnets — should be under 50 grams for reliable vertical fridge hold. If it's heavier, you need more magnets or you need to reduce frame material. Weigh a few frames and log the data; you'll see trends based on frame design and will optimize accordingly.

Polaroid frames are slightly different because the border is usually printed as a white area on the photo itself. These frames often have a tighter cavity to hide that border. The photo sits deep inside but appears borderless when viewed from the front. Test this carefully; a Polaroid that's too tight is worthless.

Instax frames are usually more forgiving. The border is already there, and the cavity usually accepts the photo easily. These are your best sellers partly because they're easier to assemble.

Collage frames with multiple photo cavities require the most attention. You're inserting multiple photos, and they need to align correctly. Do a dry assembly test first — photos inserted but nothing glued or magneted. Verify the final appearance under studio light. If it looks good, you proceed to magnets and final assembly.

Before you package for shipment, take one final look. Hold the frame at arm's length under good lighting. Rotate it slowly. Verify the back plate is flush with the main frame. Verify no paint inconsistencies. Verify the photo is straight and clean. This takes 10 seconds; it has prevented countless support tickets and negative reviews.

I use a four-tier grading system: A, B, C, and F.

A-grade is premium. This frame has zero visible layer lines at normal viewing distance — that's about 60 centimeters away. The magnets are flush, properly oriented, and hold firmly. B-grade is functional. It has minor imperfections visible only up close — less than 15 centimeters away. Maybe a tiny bit of layer line texture on the back.
