### [SCRIPT]

Alright, let's talk money. Because the beautiful thing about 3D printing magnet frames is the margin—but only if you understand your actual costs.

I'm going to build this spreadsheet live with you, so grab a piece of paper or open Google Sheets, because these numbers matter.

**First layer: Materials cost per Instax Mini frame—these are our volume sellers.**

PLA+ filament. I buy this in bulk from Printables or Prusament. The average Instax Mini frame is about 15 grams of plastic. At $20 per kilogram—and that's already a bulk discount—we're looking at **$0.30 per frame in plastic**. Don't use cheap filament here; you'll waste prints and regret it.

Magnets. I source N52 6mm x 3mm magnets in 100-packs from Aliexpress for $20/100, which is **$0.20 per frame**. Two magnets per frame. Some frames get four magnets; we'll adjust. At volume, this cost drops even more.

Super glue. Two microdots of CA glue per magnet. We're talking **$0.02 per frame**—basically free after the initial $8 bottle.

Electricity. Here's what most people forget. One hour of print time on your Bambu Lab A1 Mini costs about $0.05 per kWh in Texas. That's realistic. **$0.05 per frame.**

**Add it up: $0.57 per Instax Mini frame in raw materials.**

Now let's look at a medium-complexity build—collage frame, like the 3x3 grid we designed earlier. 

**Collage frames are where the money is.** 

This print is about 60 grams of PLA, so **$1.20 in filament**. Six magnets instead of two, **$0.60**. Same glue, **$0.04**. About 90 minutes of print time, **$0.15** in electricity.

**Total: $1.99 per collage frame.**

Now here's where the dream becomes real: **pricing**.

I sell Instax Mini frames at $10 on Shopify. Cost is $0.57. That's an $9.43 gross profit per frame, which is a **1,652% markup**. Yes. Sixteen-fifty-two.

Higher-complexity builds—the Retro TV frame, the 3x3 collage—I price at $25–$35 retail, depending on color complexity and finish.

Custom text? That's where I make real money. 30 seconds in Tinkercad to add someone's name or a date, I charge a $3–$5 upsell. They always say yes because it's a small bump on a sentimental gift.

**But hold on. Shopify takes a cut.**

Here's the brutal breakdown on a $10 sale:

- Listing fee: $0.20 (amortized across sales)
- Shopify transaction fee: 6.5% = $0.65
- Payment processing: 3% = $0.30
- Shopify Payment processing fixed: $0.25

**Total Shopify fees: ~10% of revenue, or $1.00 on a $10 sale.**

So a $10 sale nets me $8.50 after Shopify. Subtract my material cost of $0.57, and **I'm clearing $7.93 profit per frame**.

**Volume math.** This is what moved the needle for me.

- 10 frames per day: $80/day profit = $1,600/month (20 working days)
- 16 frames per day (two printers): $2,560/month
- 25 frames per day (three printers): $4,000/month

That's with zero paid ads. Just organic Shopify visibility and Instagram referrals.

Add Shopify Ads ($2–$5/day to test), and you can double that. I spend $100/month on Shopify Ads and pull in 30–40% of my revenue from ads. **ROI of 300–400%.**

The spreadsheet I'm showing you here has cells for all of this. You'll fill in your own filament costs, your local power rates, your desired profit margins. This is your north star.

## SEGMENT 2: Shopify Store Setup (~20 min)

### [INTRO]

Getting your Shopify shop live is simpler than you think, but the *details* separate shops that get buried from shops that get found. In this segment, I'll walk you through every step—shop naming, branding, and the anatomy of a high-converting product listing. I'll show you the exact title formula that got my MagnetMomentsCo shop to 300+ sales in year one, how to write descriptions that address customer questions before they ask them, and the keyword research that puts your frames in front of buyers searching "fridge magnet photo holder" instead of hoping they stumble on you.

### [SCRIPT]

Alright, you're ready to sell. Let's build your Shopify shop from zero.

**Step one: Choose your shop name.** This matters.

Your shop name should be:
- Easy to spell (no 1s for l's, no weird symbols)
- Memorable and magnet-themed if possible
- Available as a URL (you want @yourshopname to be clean)
- Not trademarked by someone else (I learned this the hard way)

Examples that work: MagnetFrameStudio, PhotoMagnetCo, FridgeArtFrames, InstaxMagnets. I have six separate brands—MackMagnets, MagnetMomentsCo, PictureThisMagnet—each targeting a different aesthetic or niche. But if you're starting? Pick one strong identity.

**Step two: Shop branding.**

You'll need:
- A banner image (1200×300px). I use Canva Pro ($13/month), which has templates. Your banner should show 2–3 of your best frames on a clean background, no clutter.
- A logo (preferably 500×500px, but Shopify accepts any square). Again, Canva. Keep it simple.
- An About section. Write 2–3 paragraphs. People buy from people. Tell them you're a hardware engineer in Austin printing custom frames, not that you're a faceless factory. Include a photo of you next to your printer—that personal touch converts.

**Step three: Create your first listing. This is where the real work is.**

The title is 140 characters. You have to pack SEO into every word.

Here's my formula: **[Frame type] + [Feature] + [Use case] + [Material]**

Let me show you mine: "3D Printed Instax Mini Frame Fridge Magnet Photo Holder PLA Gift"

Let me decode this:
- "3D Printed" = differentiator (not mass-produced plastic)
- "Instax Mini Frame" = exact product + common search term
- "Fridge Magnet" = use case (people search this)
- "Photo Holder" = what it does
- "PLA Gift" = material + occasion

Every word is searchable. That title ranks for "Instax", "fridge magnet", "photo frame", "3D printed gifts"—eight product pages worth of search terms packed into one title.

The description is 140 characters too. Here's mine verbatim:

"Handmade 3D printed frame designed for Instax Mini photos (2x3 inches). Strong N52 magnets hold your favorite moments on any metal surface. Printed in premium PLA+, sanded smooth, and ready to display. Perfect gift for Instax users, dorm decorations, or personal fridge gallery."

Structure:
- **Sentence 1:** What it is, material
- **Sentence 2:** What makes it special (strong magnets, specific photo size)
- **Sentence 3:** Condition and use case
- **Sentence 4:** Who buys it (gift buyers, dorm students, scrapbookers)

In the description tags, Shopify gives you 140 characters of product details. I use mine for dimensions:

"Frame dimensions: 3x3 inches. Photo window: 2x3 inches. Weight: ~8g. Color: [Your color]. Two N52 magnets included."

This is where customer objections die. They don't have to message you asking "does it fit my Instax photos?" because you already answered it.

**Tags. This is where most people mess up.**

Shopify gives you 13 tags. I use all 13, and I use each one strategically. Don't tag "gift ideas"—tag "instax photo frame", "fridge magnet holder", "3D printed gift", "personalized frame". 

Research your tags. Go to Shopify search, type "magnet frame", and see what pops up in autocomplete. Those are hot searches. Use eRank.io (free version) to see which tags have high search volume but low competition. This is your golden zone.

**Pricing strategy.** I start at $10 for basic Instax frames. Then I watch. If they sell out in two weeks, I raise to $12. If they sit, I keep it at $10 and boost with Shopify Ads.

**Shipping is crucial.** A magnet frame weighs ~8g. USPS First Class is $3.50–$4.50 depending on zone. I charge $4 flat shipping. On Shopify, customers see shipping upfront, so transparency wins. After my first 100 sales, I started offering free shipping on orders over $25—bulk orders love this.

**Variations: Offer them.** Same frame, different colors. That's three clicks in Shopify and it triples your perceived product range. I offer black, white, red, and gold as options.

**Photos. This is 80% of your success.** You need at minimum five high-quality photos:
1. Hero shot: frame centered, white background, slightly angled to show depth
2. Scale shot: frame in hand or next to a coin/common object
3. Lifestyle shot: frame on a fridge with magnet tested
4. Detail shot: close-up of magnet attachment and edge quality
5. Color swatch: if you offer multiple colors, show all of them

These photos need to be well-lit, in focus, and consistent. The first photo is your hero—it's the thumbnail. Make it count.

## SEGMENT 3: Product Photography (~15 min)

### [INTRO]

Product photography is where frame sellers win or lose. You can have the best magnet frame ever printed, but if your photo is flat, badly lit, or has a cluttered background, it won't sell. In this segment, I'm going to film my entire photography setup—the lightbox, the camera settings, the backgrounds—and walk you through the exact shot list for every listing. The best part? You don't need to spend $1,000 on equipment. I'm using a $20 Amazon lightbox, my iPhone 12, and free editing apps. I'll show you exactly how to get product photos that compete with Shopify shops charging 5x what you are.

### [SCRIPT]

Let me be honest: product photography intimidated me when I started. I thought I needed a DSLR, a studio, a ring light setup costing $500.

Nope.

I have an iPhone 12. A $25 portable lightbox from Amazon. A desk lamp with a 5000K bulb. And Snapseed—the free Google editing app.

That's it.

**The equipment.**

The lightbox—it's a white fabric cube, collapsible, about 24 inches on each side. It diffuses light evenly and gives you that high-end photography look without reflections or harsh shadows. I use the "Professional Photography Light Tent Kit" on Amazon—$25, includes two LED strips.

The camera: phone cameras are 2026 quality. iPhone 12 or newer, or any recent Android flagship. You're not printing billboards; you're uploading to Shopify at 2000×2000px. Your phone does that beautifully.

Lighting: natural light from a window is free and beautiful. Overcast day is ideal—diffused, no harsh shadows. If you're shooting at night, get a 5000K LED panel. I bought one for $40 on Amazon when I was scaling up. Color temperature matters—5000K looks natural, 3000K looks yellow and cheap, 6500K looks blue and weird.

Background: matte white or light gray poster board. Two dollars at Target. Tape it to the back of your lightbox. No busy backgrounds. No desk. No clutter. The frame is the star.

**The shot list. This is standardized across my six brands.**

**Shot 1: Hero shot.** Frame on white background, centered in the frame, positioned at a 45-degree angle to show depth and the magnet attachment point. This is your Shopify thumbnail. It has to pop.

**Shot 2: Scale shot.** This is critical. Frame held in someone's hand—fingers visible—so buyers know the actual size. Instax Mini frames are small. Showing a hand-holding it eliminates buyer uncertainty. If you're solo, use a stand-in's hand or a common object—a quarter, a pen, something recognizable.

**Shot 3: Lifestyle shot.** Your frame on an actual fridge with an actual photo inside. This is where the emotional sell happens. Buyers can visualize their memory on this frame on *their* fridge. Don't skip this one. It's worth 5% of your conversion rate.

**Shot 4: Detail shot.** Close-up, macro focus. Show the magnet attachment, the smooth edges (if you sanded), the material texture. This answers the quality question: "Is this crude or professional?"

**Shot 5: Color family.** If you offer black, white, red, and gold—show all four on one white background. This image doesn't go in listing photos; it goes in your shop banner. It positions you as someone with options and range.

**Editing workflow.** You shoot RAW if your phone supports it (iPhone shoots HEIC, which is fine). Import to Snapseed (free on iOS/Android).

Adjustments in this order:
1. **Brightness:** bump +15–+25. Product shots should be bright and airy.
2. **Contrast:** +10 makes edges pop.
3. **Saturation:** actually, leave it alone. If your frame is white, let it be white. Don't oversaturate.
4. **White balance:** if the background looks gray instead of white, drag the temperature dial warm by +3–+5.
5. **Sharpen:** +20–+30. Crisp edges make it look professional.
6. **Crop:** Shopify prefers square 2000×2000px. Your phone shoots 16:9. Crop to square, centered on the frame.

Export at 2000×2000px PNG. You're done.

**Frequency: shoot once a week.** Set aside 30 minutes every Saturday morning. Take your new designs, pop them in the lightbox, shoot all five angles, edit them all at once, upload to Shopify. Batch efficiency.

**Content series:** Beyond the five product angles, I also shoot:
- Progress shots: frame halfway through assembly
- Print timelapses: (use Bambu Labs' built-in camera)
- Gratuitule unboxings: customer feedback video

These go on Instagram and TikTok to drive traffic *back* to your Shopify listings.

## SEGMENT 4: Scaling Production (~20 min)

### [INTRO]

There's a fascinating moment that happens around week 4–6 when your Shopify shop goes from experimental to promising. You're selling 3–5 frames per day. Your first printer is running nearly 24/7. And you realize: you've hit the ceiling on what one machine can do. This segment maps the exact progression from single-printer hobby to multi-printer business—stage by stage costs, timelines, the printer models I recommend, and the batch workflow that lets you go from managing prints to managing an actual operation. I'll show you real timelines from my businesses: how I went from Month 1 (one printer) to Month 12 (six brands, four printers, $50K annual revenue).

### [SCRIPT]

Here's what I wish someone told me early: one printer isn't a business. One printer is a really good hobby.

Let me map the stages I've gone through, because you will too.

**Stage One: Solo Printer (Month 1–2)**

You have one Bambu Lab A1 Mini ($199) or Prusa Mini+ ($349). You're learning. You're printing 4–8 frames per day. Your turnaround is 2–3 days. You're profitable—you're clearing $300–$500/month—but you're also in every step: printing, removing supports, sanding, assembly, packing, shipping.

Your bottleneck is print time. An Instax frame takes 45 minutes. A collage frame takes 90 minutes. If you want to print two simultaneously, you need two printers, or you're waiting.

**Stage Two: Dual Printers (Month 3–4)**

This is the critical expansion. You buy a second printer. I recommend the Bambu Lab A1 Mini or the used Prusa i3 MK3S+. The A1 Mini is $199 and is *insanely* reliable for small frames. The MK3S+ is $350 used and is the workhorse if you're doing larger complex prints.

Now you're printing 8–16 frames per day. Your revenue jumps to $1,000–$2,500/month (depending on ASP and Shopify Ads spend). Your biggest bottleneck is no longer print time—it's **post-processing**.

This is where most people quit. They don't realize that a print comes off the build plate looking 60% done. You have to:
- Remove supports (3–5 minutes per frame)
- Sand edges smooth (2–3 minutes)
- Clean off support residue (1 minute)
- Attach magnets with super glue (1 minute)
- Let glue cure (10 minutes, but batched, so negligible)
- Package and address label (2 minutes)

That's 9–12 minutes of hands-on work per frame. 12 frames a day = 2 hours of post-processing. That *could* be you, or it's time to bring in help.

If you're doing this solo at 16 frames/day, you're spending 3+ hours daily on post-processing. That's unsustainable.

**Stage Three: 3–5 Printers + Help (Month 6–12)**

This is where I am with my six brands. I have 3–5 printers running on staggered schedules. Some are running overnight (11 PM–7 AM), so I wake up to finished prints. In the morning, I'm only doing post-processing. By afternoon, I'm packing and shipping. By evening, I'm loading the next batch.

My inventory system is a simple Google Sheet:
- Filament stock (5kg+ in main colors)
- Magnet stock (1000+ magnets in bins)
- Frame designs (ready to print: instax, collage, retro TV, custom, etc.)
- Orders pending (date ordered → ship date → shipped)
- Printed frames ready to ship (count by color, count by type)

Revenue at this stage: $3,000–$6,000/month per brand. I have six brands. Do the math.

**The workflow:** Here's how I batch-print to maximize machine efficiency.

Morning (8 AM): I prep the printer. I have three Bambu Lab printers set up on a shelf in my office. I check:
- Filament levels (rethread if needed)
- Nozzle temperature (set to filament specs—PLA+ is 220°C)
- Build plate clean (wipe with alcohol, wait 30s)

I load a 4-frame plate into Bambu Studio (their software). One Instax frame in each corner. This takes 40 minutes total, uses 60g of filament. I hit print and walk away.

Early afternoon (1 PM): The first batch is done. Printer one is unloading. Printer two is still printing the overnight run. Printer three has just started a 90-minute collage batch. I now have 2 hours to post-process the morning batch.

I remove supports with a precision knife (2 minutes × 4 frames = 8 minutes). I sand all edges on an orbital sander with 120-grit sandpaper (15 seconds per edge × 4 frames = 2 minutes total because I batch this). I inspect for quality. I glue magnets on and let them cure while I move to the next printer's output.

Late afternoon (4 PM): I'm packing. I have my packing station: small boxes, mailers, labels, tissue paper. Printer two is done and cooling. Printer three is halfway through the collage batch.

Evening (6 PM): I load the next overnight batch. This usually runs midnight to 8 AM. Meanwhile, I'm answering customer messages, updating inventory, maybe filming Reel content for Instagram from today's prints.

This workflow lets me produce 24–32 frames per day with ~4 hours of active work from me. The machines do the heavy lifting.

**Printer recommendation by stage:**

- **Stage 1:** Bambu Lab A1 Mini ($199). Reliability is 99.8%. It's a workhorse. Or Prusa Mini+ ($349) if you want a bit more precision for larger frames.
- **Stage 2:** Same printer again, or: Bambu Lab A1 ($799). Larger build plate, faster printing, same reliability.
- **Stage 3:** Bambu Lab X1 Carbon ($999—used market has these at $700–$800). Massive bed, AMS automatic material loading, perfect for high volume.

I'd skip expensive industrial printers until you're at 100+ frames/day. Prusa XL, FormLabs, MakerBot—these are $3k+. You don't need them yet.

**Filament management:**

I buy in bulk. A 5-roll bundle of Bambu Lab PLA+ is $60 ($12/roll). A single roll is $20. That's 40% price break for bulk ordering every 2 months.

I track usage:
- Black PLA+: 5kg/month
- White PLA+: 3kg/month
- RGB (red, green, blue, gold): 1kg/month each

I store filament in dry-box containers with desiccant packs. PLA is hygroscopic; wet filament prints badly. This never happened to me because I organized early.

**Seasonal demand:**

Here's the reality nobody talks about: your sales aren't linear.

- June–August: slow. People are outside, not buying. 50% of baseline.
- September–October: moderate pickup. Back to school. New dorm students.
- November–December: *explosion*. Holiday shopping. 3–5x baseline.
- January: crash. Post-holiday slump. 70% of baseline.
- February–May: steady climb back.

In October, I print 200 extra frames and inventory them. In November, I'm shipping 60+ frames daily. In January, I'm still profitable because I'm using October's inventory.

**When to hire help:**

When your post-processing time exceeds 3 hours per day, or when you hit 20+ orders per day that you can't physically pack in 4 hours, hire someone.

I hired my first assistant in Month 8 when I was at 50 frames/day across two of my brands. I pay $18/hour for post-processing (support removal, sanding, magnet glue). That assistant does 20 frames in 4 hours, which justifies the cost at my ASP. I went from working 5 hours a day to working 2 hours a day while revenue stayed the same because I'd unblocked myself.

## SEGMENT 5: Marketing & Social Media (~15 min)

### [INTRO]

You've built something beautiful and you've optimized your Shopify shop, but if nobody knows about you, Shopify's algorithm won't help you either. Here's what I learned: Shopify is a search engine, and like Google, it rewards traffic. Bring them customers, and Shopify rewards you with visibility. In this segment, I'm walking through the exact social media playbook I used to take MagnetMomentsCo from 0 to 5,000 followers in 6 months. Instagram Reels, TikTok, Pinterest—each platform has a different role in your funnel. I'll show you the content types that get 100k+ impressions, the posting frequency that works, and how to measure what's actually converting to sales. You'll also see my Shopify Ads strategy ($1–$5/day test budgets) and why customer reviews matter infinitely more than paid ads.

### [SCRIPT]

Social media is the second-order lever. Shopify organic gets you 30% of sales. Social media gets you the other 70%.

Let me walk you through my actual feeds.

**Instagram: 3x per week minimum.**

Your followers are people who either own Instax Mini cameras or collect photo frames or are into 3D-printed gifts. You speak to that person.

Content types that work for me:

**Time-lapse videos:** 15–30 second video of a frame printing and being assembled, sped up 4x. No music needed. The satisfying *bzzzzt* of supports being removed? That's your hook. I post these as Stories and Reels. These get 50k–100k impressions each. Comment: "Satisfying assembly ✨ Fresh print just came off the plate. DM for custom requests. Link in bio."

**Before/after:** Print fresh off the plate (rough, white, boring) → fully sanded and magnetized → packaged and shipped. Three photos in a carousel. Comment: "The glow-up. Raw print → finished frame. This baby ships tomorrow. Which color would you choose? 🖼️" These drive 30k–40k impressions.

## SEGMENT 6: Handling Custom Orders (~15 min)

### [INTRO]

Custom orders are the secret weapon of 3D print businesses. They're higher margin, higher margin, and higher emotional stake for buyers—meaning they also have higher review potential if you nail it. But they're also a potential time sink landmine if you don't have the workflow locked down. In this segment, I'm walking you through the real custom requests I get weekly—dog faces, corporate logos, personalized text, specific sizes—and the exact playbook I use to scope, price, design, and deliver them. You'll see my templated messaging, my TinkerCAD workflow for quick design iterations, and the calculator that determines whether a custom request is worth your time. Custom orders can be 20–40% of your revenue if you marketing them right—and I'll show you how to do that at the end.

### [SCRIPT]

Here's the conversation I have with custom requests at least three times a week:

**Customer message:** "Hi Ajaya! I have a photo of my dog and my partner. Can you make a frame that holds both? It's for her birthday. She loves Instax so I found you. Budget is $20–$30. She needs it by next Wednesday."

This is a custom request. It has several variables: specific photo dimensions, specific deadline, specific price expectation, specific emotional weight (it's a gift).

Here's my response (I have this templated in Shopify's notes):

---

"Hi [Name],

Thanks so much for thinking of me for this! I love this idea. Here's what I can do:

I can create a dual frame that holds two Instax Mini photos side-by-side (3×6 inches total). I'll print it in your choice of color (black, white, red, or gold), and include magnets so it sticks to any metal surface. Design + print + assembly would be 3–5 business days. 

Pricing: Base frame ($15) + custom dual design ($15) = $30 total. Shipping is $4.

Does this work for you? If yes, I'll need:
1. The photo dimensions (send a screenshot or dimensions)
2. Your color preference
3. Confirm the 3–5 day timeline works for you

Once I hear back, I'll send you a custom Shopify listing to pay for, and I'll start the print immediately.

Thanks! — Ajaya"

---

Notice what I did here:

1. I **scoped the request** (dual frame, Instax dimensions)
2. I **set the timeline** (3–5 business days, not "by Wednesday" if Wednesday is 2 days away)
3. I **priced it** (base + design fee + shipping)
4. I **confirmed the variables** (color, dimensions, deadline)

I don't start designing until they pay and confirm.

**The pricing framework:**

- **Base frame** = my standard frame price for that category. Instax = $8–$10. A collage = $20.
- **Design fee** = $10–$15 for simple customization (color, text). $20–$30 for complex (logo embedding, multi-photo layout, irregular shape).
- **Complexity surcharge** = -$0 if it's standard geometry. +$5 if it requires significant CAD work or multiple iterations.

So:
- Custom text on Instax frame: $10 (base) + $5 (design) = $15
- Dual-photo frame: $15 (base) + $15 (design) = $30
- Company logo embedded: $20 (base) + $30 (design) = $50

I price conservatively for one reason: **the first iteration might be wrong.** If they ask for changes, revision is free (1–2 iterations). If they ask for fundamental changes after two iterations, that's a new design fee.

**The design workflow in TinkerCAD:**

I have a library of frame designs that I've parameterized. "Instax Mini frame" is my template. I duplicate it, modify:
- Photo window dimensions
- Color
- Text positioning (if custom text)
- Magnet hole placement (if different size)

I export as STL, run it through my Bambu Studio slicer, check the preview (45 seconds), and I'm done. A simple custom taking me ~8 minutes in design + 45 minutes to print + 12 minutes to post-process = 65 minutes total. At $30, that's $27/hour profit on custom work. Beats my baseline Instax frame ($7.93 profit in 15 minutes of labor, or $31.72/hour... actually, wait, custom is the same rate but with upfront design risk. Let me recalculate: custom is 65 minutes labor, $30 profit = $27.69/hour. Standard Instax is 15 minutes labor, $7.93 profit = $31.72/hour. So standard frames are actually *better* rate, but custom orders have higher ASP and are more defensible (customer can't price-compare as easily). I still do custom because it builds relationships.)

**When to say no:**

I get custom requests I turn down:

- **Impossible physics:** "Can you make a frame that's 1mm thick and hold a 200g photo?" No. PLA minimum wall thickness is 2–3mm. I explain why.
- **Unreasonable timeline:** "It's due tomorrow." No. I set minimum 3 business days to keep my sanity.
- **Unreasonable price:** "Can you do this amazing 3D-printed display stand for $15?" That's undervaluing. I counter-offer with realistic pricing.
- **Very niche low-volume:** "Can you print 500 of these?" If it's a one-time bulk order, I take it if it's profitable (3–5 printer run at maybe $2,000 revenue). If it's recurring, I recommend they source direct from a factory in China—I can't compete on volume.

I've turned down maybe 10 requests in 2 years. The other 200+ I've done, and most have been profitable and resulted in repeat customers.

**Documentation: Save every custom design.**

Every custom design I create gets saved in my "2026 Custom Gallery" folder, labeled "[Customer Name] - [Design] - [Date]". I have 200+ designs now. Why?

50% of my repeat customers ask for variations of existing designs. "Can you do that dog frame again but in white instead of black?" That's a 2-minute modification. I quote $20 (base) + $5 (variation) = $25, I spend 7 minutes, I'm done. That's $214/hour profit.

**Shopify messaging system:**

I enable custom order messaging so customers can ask questions before buying. I have templated responses for:
- "Do you do [specific request]?" → "Yes! [scope explanation] [timeline] [pricing]. Interested? Message me with details."
- "How long does it take?" → "Standard designs: 3–5 business days. Complex custom: 5–7 business days. Rush available for +$15."
- "Can I get a preview?" → "Absolutely! Once I design it, I'll send you a photo of the STL rendered in TinkerCAD before printing."
- "What if I don't like it?" → "I'll revise up to 2 times free. After that, additional changes are a new design fee."

These templates save me 1–2 hours per week of customer communication.

**Marketing custom work:**

Most people don't think to ask if you do custom. I made a separate Instagram Reel showing 5 custom builds timelapse'd together (20 seconds). Caption: "We do custom 🎨 Got an idea? DM or shop link in bio". That Reel got 60k impressions and resulted in 12 custom inquiries that month. 12 custom orders at $30 average = $360 incremental revenue.

Now my bio says: "Custom magnet frames 🎨" and my custom request rate has gone from 1 per month to 2–3 per week.
