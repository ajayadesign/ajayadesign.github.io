12 Must-Haves for Your Restaurant Website in 2026

It's Friday night. Someone pulls out their phone and Googles "best Thai food near me." Your restaurant pops up. They tap through to your website and… it takes 6 seconds to load. The menu is a blurry PDF. The hours might be from 2023. There's no way to order online. They hit the back button and choose the restaurant below you — the one with the clean, fast site and a big "Order Now" button.

This happens hundreds of times a week to restaurants with bad websites. And the worst part? You never know you lost that customer. They just… disappear.

In 2026, your restaurant website isn't just a digital business card. It's your host, your menu, your ordering system, and your first impression — all in one. Here are the 12 features that separate restaurants thriving online from those bleeding customers to competitors.

1. Online Ordering (Non-Negotiable)

If you're still relying 100% on third-party apps like DoorDash and Uber Eats, you're giving away 15-30% of every order in commissions. That's $15,000–$30,000/year for a restaurant doing $100K in delivery sales.

Your website needs its own ordering system. First-party online ordering lets you keep the full margin, own the customer data, and build direct relationships. Platforms like Square Online, ChowNow, and Toast offer ordering widgets you can embed directly on your site. The cost? $0–$100/month — a fraction of what you're paying DoorDash.

That doesn't mean abandon the apps entirely — they're great for discovery. But your website should be where regulars order. Train them with incentives: "Order directly from our website and get 10% off."

2. Mobile-First Design

Over 80% of restaurant website traffic comes from mobile devices. Not 50%. Not 60%. Eighty percent. If your site doesn't look perfect on a phone, you've already lost the majority of your visitors.

Mobile-first means: big tap targets (at least 44×44px), readable text without zooming, fast load times on cellular connections, and menus that are easy to scroll on a 6-inch screen. Test your site on an actual phone — not just your browser's mobile simulator.

3. An HTML Menu (Not a PDF)

This is the single most common restaurant website mistake — and it's costing you customers and SEO rankings.

PDF menus are terrible on mobile (pinch-to-zoom, slow to load, often unreadable). They're invisible to Google — search engines can't easily index PDF content, so your menu items won't appear in search results. And they're inaccessible to screen readers, which violates ADA compliance guidelines.

Build your menu as an HTML page with proper headings, descriptions, prices, and categories. It's better for users, better for Google, and better for accessibility. Bonus: add menu schema markup so Google can display your dishes directly in search results.

4. Professional Food Photography

People eat with their eyes first — especially online. A study by MGH found that 45% of diners try a new restaurant because they saw appealing photos of the food. Your cousin's iPhone photos under fluorescent kitchen lights aren't cutting it.

Invest in a professional food photography session. It costs $300–$800 for a set of 20-30 high-quality images, and those photos will work harder for you than any other marketing investment. Use them on your website, social media, Google Business Profile, and menu boards.

If professional photography isn't in the budget yet, at least follow these rules: natural lighting only, clean backgrounds, overhead or 45-degree angles, and no flash. Ever.

5. Accurate Hours & Location

This sounds obvious, but you'd be amazed how many restaurant websites show wrong hours. Someone drives 20 minutes to your restaurant only to find you're closed? They'll never come back — and they'll leave a one-star review telling everyone about it.

Display your hours prominently — ideally in the header or above the fold on every page. Include holiday hours and any seasonal changes. Add an embedded Google Map with your exact pin location. And keep these synced with your Google Business Profile — inconsistencies confuse both customers and Google's algorithm.

6. Reservation/Waitlist System

If your restaurant takes reservations, your website should be the easiest way to make one. Integrate OpenTable, Resy, Yelp Reservations, or even a simple form. The key is reducing friction — every extra step between "I want to eat there" and "I'm booked" loses customers.

For casual restaurants without reservations, consider a waitlist feature. Services like Waitwhile or Yelp Waitlist let customers join your line remotely and get text updates. It reduces walk-away rates by 20-30%.

7. Reviews & Social Proof

92% of consumers read online reviews before visiting a restaurant. Display your best reviews prominently on your website. Pull in Google reviews, Yelp ratings, or curated testimonials. Include specific details — "The pad thai is the best I've had outside Thailand" is 10x more convincing than "Great food!"

Don't fake it. Don't cherry-pick only 5-star reviews. Show a mix of genuine, detailed reviews with your aggregate rating. Customers are sophisticated — they trust 4.5 stars more than 5.0 because they know nobody's perfect.

8. Page Speed Under 3 Seconds

Google data shows that 53% of mobile visitors abandon a site that takes longer than 3 seconds to load. For restaurants, where people are often searching while hungry and impatient, that threshold is probably even lower.

The biggest speed killers on restaurant websites: unoptimized food photos (a single DSLR photo can be 5-10MB), heavy video backgrounds, uncompressed scripts, and slow hosting. Compress images to WebP format, use lazy loading, and choose performance-focused hosting. Run your site through our speed test tool to see where you stand.

9. Local SEO Optimization

When someone searches "best pizza in [your city]," you want to be in that top 3 map pack. Your website plays a critical role in local SEO:

  • NAP consistency: Your Name, Address, and Phone number must be identical everywhere — website, Google Business, Yelp, Facebook, all directories
  • Location pages: If you have multiple locations, each gets its own page with unique content
  • Local schema markup: Restaurant structured data helps Google understand your business type, cuisine, price range, and hours
  • Local keywords: Include your city/neighborhood naturally throughout your content
  • Google Business Profile link: Link to it and keep it updated with posts, photos, and menu items

10. Easy Contact & Directions

Make it dead simple to call you, email you, or get directions. A click-to-call button on mobile is essential — no copying phone numbers. An embedded Google Map with a "Get Directions" link that opens in the user's map app. A simple contact form for catering inquiries, event bookings, and feedback.

11. Email/SMS Capture

Your website should be building your marketing list with every visit. Offer a simple incentive — "Join our list for a free appetizer" or "Get exclusive deals" — in exchange for an email or phone number. This is your most valuable marketing asset because you own it. Social media algorithms change. Google rankings fluctuate. But your email list is yours forever.

Keep it simple: name and email (or phone). No lengthy forms. Use a pop-up that triggers on exit intent or after 30 seconds on site. Tools like Mailchimp, Constant Contact, or even Square's built-in email marketing work great for restaurants.

12. Catering & Events Information

If you offer catering or host private events, this should have its own dedicated page — not a footnote on your About page. Catering inquiries are high-value orders (often $500–$5,000+) and deserve a clear, compelling presentation.

Include: catering menu options, minimum order sizes, delivery area, lead time requirements, a contact form specifically for catering, and photos from previous events. Make it easy for office managers and event planners to say yes.

The Bottom Line

Your restaurant website is working for you 24 hours a day — or it's working against you. In 2026, customers expect fast, mobile-friendly, visually appealing sites with online ordering as a baseline. Everything beyond that is what separates you from the competition.

You don't need all 12 features on day one. Start with the non-negotiables: mobile-first design, HTML menu, accurate hours, and online ordering. Then build up. But whatever you do, don't let a bad website be the reason good food goes undiscovered.
