How Much Does a Small Business Website Cost in 2026? A Transparent Breakdown

"How much does a website cost?" is the single most-Googled question in web design — and the most poorly answered. Most articles give you a range so wide it's useless: "$500 to $50,000." Thanks for nothing.

This article is different. I'm going to give you real numbers, explain what drives the price up or down, call out the hidden costs nobody tells you about, and help you figure out exactly what you should spend based on your business size and goals. No fluff. No sales pitch. Just facts.

The Quick Answer

For a typical small business (under 50 employees, local service area), here's what you should expect to pay in 2026:

  • DIY website builder (Wix, Squarespace, GoDaddy): $192–$552/year ($16–$46/month)
  • WordPress with a freelancer: $1,500–$5,000 build + $600–$2,400/year maintenance
  • Custom-coded site (independent designer): $2,000–$8,000 build + $0–$600/year hosting
  • Agency build: $5,000–$25,000+ build + $1,200–$6,000/year retainer

Those numbers tell part of the story. The real cost depends on what you're actually getting — and what you're giving up.

Option 1: DIY Website Builders ($16–$46/month)

What you get: A drag-and-drop editor, templates, basic hosting, and an SSL certificate. Platforms like Wix, Squarespace, and GoDaddy Website Builder are the most popular choices.

What you don't get: Performance. SEO flexibility. Custom functionality. Ownership.

Let me explain that last point, because it's the one that catches most business owners off guard. When you build on Wix or Squarespace, you don't own your website. You're renting space on their platform. If they raise prices, change features, or shut down, your site goes with them. You can't export a Wix site and move it somewhere else. You're locked in.

Performance is the other issue. Template-based builders typically score 40-65 on Google Lighthouse (the industry benchmark for website quality). That's a D or F grade. These platforms load unnecessary JavaScript, bloated CSS frameworks, and tracking scripts that slow your site to a crawl. For reference, Google recommends a Lighthouse score above 90 for good search ranking signals.

Best for: Hobbies, personal blogs, or very early-stage businesses testing an idea before investing.

Not ideal for: Any business that depends on being found in Google search, or any business that wants to grow beyond its current size.

Option 2: WordPress + Freelancer ($1,500–$5,000 + ongoing)

What you get: A WordPress site built by a freelancer, usually on a premium theme with some customization. You get more flexibility than DIY builders, access to thousands of plugins, and (in theory) you own the site.

The hidden costs:

  • Hosting: $10–$50/month for decent hosting (SiteGround, WP Engine). Cheap hosting ($3/month) = slow site.
  • Premium plugins: Forms, SEO tools, page builders, security — $100–$500/year in plugin licenses.
  • Maintenance: WordPress needs constant updates. Core updates, plugin updates, security patches. If you skip them, your site breaks or gets hacked. Budget $50–$200/month for maintenance, or learn to do it yourself.
  • Security: WordPress powers 43% of the web, which also makes it the #1 target for hackers. You'll need a security plugin, regular backups, and someone monitoring for vulnerabilities.

The quality gamble: Freelancer quality varies enormously. Some are excellent professionals who deliver amazing work. Others install a $59 theme, swap out the logo, and call it "custom." Ask to see their portfolio and specifically ask about performance scores.

Best for: Businesses that need a blog, e-commerce, or complex functionality and have the budget for ongoing maintenance.

Option 3: Custom-Coded Website ($2,000–$8,000)

What you get: A website built from scratch — hand-coded HTML, CSS, and JavaScript (or modern frameworks). No WordPress, no templates, no bloat. Every line of code serves a purpose.

Why it performs better: A custom-coded site typically loads 3-5x faster than WordPress or template sites. No unused plugins, no bloated themes, no database queries for every page load. These sites routinely score 95-100 on Google Lighthouse.

The ownership advantage: You own every file. Your site can be hosted anywhere — GitHub Pages (free), Netlify (free), Cloudflare Pages (free), or any hosting provider. No monthly platform fees. No lock-in. If you want to switch designers or maintain it yourself, you can.

The ongoing costs:

  • Hosting: $0–$20/month. Static sites can be hosted for free on platforms like GitHub Pages or Cloudflare Pages.
  • Domain: $10–$15/year.
  • Maintenance: Minimal. No plugins to update, no CMS to patch. Content updates are the main ongoing need.

Best for: Small businesses that want maximum performance, minimum ongoing costs, and full ownership of their website.

Option 4: Agency Build ($5,000–$25,000+)

What you get: A full-service experience: strategy, branding, copywriting, design, development, SEO setup, and usually a monthly retainer for ongoing work.

When it makes sense: If you're doing $500K+ in annual revenue and need a complex site (e-commerce with 100+ products, multi-location business, custom web applications), an agency can deliver the full package.

When it doesn't make sense: If you're a local business with a 5-page site. You're paying for project managers, account managers, and office overhead that has nothing to do with your website's quality. A skilled independent designer can deliver equal or better results at a fraction of the price.

The Hidden Costs Nobody Mentions

Beyond the sticker price, watch out for these:

  • Stock photos: $50–$500. Some designers use free stock (Unsplash, Pexels), others charge for premium stock.
  • Copywriting: If you can't write your own content, professional copywriting runs $50–$200 per page.
  • Email setup: Google Workspace or Microsoft 365 for professional email (@yourbusiness.com) is $6–$12/user/month.
  • SSL certificate: Free with most modern hosting (Let's Encrypt). If someone charges you for SSL in 2026, walk away.
  • SEO: Basic on-page SEO should be included. Ongoing SEO (content marketing, link building, technical audits) is a separate service: $500–$2,000/month.
  • Analytics: Google Analytics is free. But setting it up properly (goals, conversions, event tracking) takes expertise.

How to Decide What to Spend

Here's a simple framework:

  1. Calculate your customer lifetime value (CLV). How much is one customer worth to you over a year? For a restaurant, maybe $500. For a dentist, maybe $3,000. For a home remodeler, maybe $15,000.
  2. Estimate how many customers a good website could bring. Conservatively, a professional website with local SEO can bring 5-20 new customers per month for a local business.
  3. Do the math. If your CLV is $500 and a website brings 5 new customers/month, that's $2,500/month in new revenue. A $3,000 website pays for itself in 6 weeks.

The question isn't "can I afford a website?" It's "can I afford not to have a good one?"
